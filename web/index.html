    Your files stay with you

    Everything happens in JavaScript, which runs in your browser, on your own computer. Your PDFs are never uploaded or sent anywhere.

    You can check: once this page has loaded, turn off your Wi-Fi and drop a PDF. It works the same.

    What it does

    Law review articles, Supreme Court opinions, and book chapters have large margins when printed on letter paper: the text was set for a much smaller page. Printing two pages per sheet the usual way shrinks those margins along with the text, so you get tiny print surrounded by blank paper.

    This measures where the text actually is and trims the margins away before placing the pages side by side, so it usually prints close to its original size, on half the paper.

    A document that already fills a full-size page has little to trim. You still get the file but with a warning if the text comes out below 85% of its original size.

    Why “concur”?

    Today, to concur is mostly to agree, as in a concurring opinion. But the word comes from the Latin concurrere, “to run together” (con-, together, and currere, to run), and for centuries that is what it meant in English: things concurred when they came together, met at a point, or happened at once. Concurrent has meant “running together side by side” since the late 1400s.

    That older sense is still around. In law, sentences run concurrently and two courts can have concurrent jurisdiction. In software, tasks are concurrent when a computer has them under way side by side. And here, it is what happens to your pages.

    Some older uses:

    • Shakespeare, Troilus and Cressida (c. 1602): As Hector’s leisure and your bounties shall / Concur together
    • Burton, The Anatomy of Melancholy (1621), on the eye: those optic nerves, concurring both in one
    • Hooke, Micrographia (1665), on rays of starlight: they will again concurr and meet, in the point M
    • Blackstone, Commentaries on the Laws of England (1765), on two statutes that can “both stand together”: they shall both have a concurrent efficacy
    • Hamilton, The Federalist No. 32 (1788): The necessity of a concurrent jurisdiction in certain cases results from the division of the sovereign power
    • Webster’s dictionary (1828), first definition: To meet in the same point

    The Online Etymology Dictionary has the full history.
